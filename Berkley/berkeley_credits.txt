Association for the Advancement of Sustainability in Higher Education
On behalf of the University of California, Berkeley, I am pleased to submit our documentation for AASHE’s Sustainability Tracking, Assessment, and Rating System (STARS), version 3.0. This submission represents a comprehensive and accurate account of our sustainability initiatives to the best of our knowledge at the time of submission.
UC Berkeley’s 150-year commitment to the greater good drives our renowned scholarship and environmental solutions, making us one of the greenest public higher education institutions in the world. Deep campus engagement in environmental issues has been integral to our progress in sustainability. We are especially proud of the achievements of our students, faculty, and staff, which reflect our enduring spirit of innovation.
Our core mission of teaching, research, and public service provides a unique opportunity to use the campus as a living laboratory for sustainability. UC Berkeley is consistently rated the top school in the world for environmental sciences, with departments across the campus—not just the renowned Rausser College of Natural Resources—producing groundbreaking scholarship. Offering over 900 sustainability-focused courses, we emphasize integrating sustainability across disciplines to foster sustainability literacy among our students.
Our research laboratories are world leaders in advancing our understanding of climate change, as well as developing energy, environmental, and social solutions to address global challenges in the decades to come. Meanwhile, UC Berkeley’s vibrant environmental community embodies a diverse and inclusive culture of sustainability, celebrating diverse narratives while fostering problem-solving, interdisciplinarity, and leadership. Over 50 sustainability-related student groups and countless faculty initiatives engage in activities that drive institutional change.
UC Berkeley’s sustainability commitment is reflected in our operations. We are accelerating a just and equitable transition to a resilient, clean energy, zero-carbon campus. This includes the design of a transformational project to replace our cogeneration heating infrastructure with a fully electrified system, dramatically reducing reliance on fossil fuels. We are on track to eliminate single-use plastics and offer viable alternatives by 2030, and we have infused sustainability into dining services with third-party-certified sustainably sourced meat, seafood, and plant-forward menus. Additionally, our campus lands are managed with virtually no chemicals or pesticides, demonstrating a holistic approach to sustainability.
Throughout its history, UC Berkeley has stepped up to meet the challenges of the moment. Today, we continue to lead by setting ambitious goals and implementing cutting-edge strategies to address the pressing carbon reduction and clean energy transitions required.
Our participation in STARS 3.0 reflects our commitment to advancing sustainability on campus and beyond. This version’s more rigorous framework underscores our dedication to tackling complex sustainability challenges while holding ourselves accountable to the highest standards. Building on the success of our previous STARS submission, we are proud to showcase our continued progress while identifying areas for future growth. This process highlights UC Berkeley’s efforts to integrate sustainability into our operations, academics, and community engagement.
Thank you for the opportunity to partner with AASHE as we work toward making higher education a model for the transformative change our world urgently requires.

UC Berkeley has been a leader in climate action for more than 15 years. Advancing its efforts further, the campus announced the Berkeley Clean Energy Campus, an ambitious project that will be its most significant climate effort to date. The project will create an entirely new energy microgrid for the campus that will be fully electric and largely eliminate the largest source of campus-related carbon emissions, a natural gas-fueled cogeneration plant that is nearing end-of-life. The new campus energy system will support increased operations, enrollment, and research efforts well into the future.
The core of the Berkeley Clean Energy Campus project is replacement of the existing natural gas-fueled cogeneration plant with an electrified heat recovery system, including a centralized heating plant, geothermal wells, and thermal energy storage. A campus-wide steam distribution network from the cogeneration plant would be decommissioned and replaced with new hot and chilled water loops that provide more efficient heating and cooling to campus buildings. The buildings themselves will be converted to allow for the new water supply. Future phases of the Berkeley Clean Energy Campus will allow for implementation of distributed energy resources, including solar PV, battery storage, and generators, providing the campus far greater resilience capabilities.
The Berkeley Clean Energy Campus aligns with the State of California’s and the University of California’s aggressive climate goals. The project will serve as a global model and demonstrate that large-scale greenhouse gas reductions can be successfully achieved in a relatively short time frame. The Integrated Resource and Activation Plan (IRAP) envisions this transformation could be undertaken in two phases that would reduce campus carbon emissions (Scope 1 and Scope 2) 85% by 2035.
Supported by a $249 million lead capital investment by the State of California, the Berkeley Clean Energy Campus project is progressing on schedule. Design and engineering studies are underway. The UC Board of Regents has approved pre-construction funding for the first phase and, following approval of the project’s final design and environmental review, construction is slated to begin in 2026.

In April 2020, then-UC Berkeley Chancellor Carol Christ announced the country's most ambitious commitment to eliminating single-use plastics. The initiative aims to phase out all non-essential single-use plastics with viable alternatives by 2030. Unlike typical plastic bans that target items like bottles, foodware, and plastic bags, UC Berkeley's approach addresses a broader range of products and packaging used across academics, research, administration, giveaways, and events. This includes commonly overlooked items such as chip bags, laboratory plastics, and packaging. By tackling these challenges, the initiative promotes upstream circular economy solutions, transitions to reusable alternatives, and reinforces UC Berkeley’s leadership in sustainability and zero-waste practices.
The campus has formed a Single Use Plastics Working Group made up of diverse campus stakeholders to develop key strategies in purchasing, contracts, and tasked to create a roadmap to meet the 2030 goal.

Herbicide-Free UC works to eliminate the use of toxic herbicides across all University of California campuses through three main strategies: Gardener Support & Engagement, Student Education, and Research. The campaign empowers students to build strong relationships with campus gardeners, understand landscaping needs, and provide the specific information and resources gardeners require to reduce herbicide use. It also offers support in the form of data, student engagement, financial assistance, and training in alternative maintenance methods.To institutionalize these efforts, Herbicide-Free UC helps develop consensus-based policies and a system-wide best practices guide to support sustainable landscaping at UC campuses. Herbicide-Free Campus was co-founded by Mackenzie Feldman and Bridget Gustafson at UC Berkeley in 2017. Their advocacy led to a major milestone in May 2019, when the University of California banned glyphosate-based herbicides at all UC locations. UC President Janet Napolitano cited "concerns about possible human health and ecological hazards" as the reason for this decision.
Since then, UC Berkeley’s Landscape Services department has eliminated the use of glyphosate-based herbicides and minimized the use of all other herbicides. For the past three years, herbicide use on the core campus has been nearly eliminated. Instead, gardeners and the Landscape Services team manage weeds through alternative methods such as sheet mulching, hand-pulling, fogging, and seasonal weed whipping. These efforts are supported by student groups, volunteers, and contractors who assist with manual weed removal.
Additionally, the department exclusively uses organic fertilizers for turf maintenance, and 10 gardeners have earned certification in regenerative landscape management, directly advancing Herbicide-Free UC’s goals of education and training. Herbicide-free practices across the UC system are now overseen by the Systemwide Pesticide Oversight Committee (SPOC). This committee, which includes experts in agriculture, toxicology, environmental health, sustainability, and facilities management, implements the recommendations of the 2020 Herbicide Task Force report and manages UC’s Integrated Pest Management Policy. SPOC also monitors pesticide use and reports on system-wide practices.UC Berkeley’s leadership in sustainable landscaping was featured on KPFA 94.1’s Terra Verde show on November 22, 2024. During the interview, Lydia Woltjer and Mackenzie Feldman highlighted organic landscaping practices and how they have shifted the campus toward regenerative and sustainable solutions. Together, Herbicide-Free UC, gardeners, and the Landscape Services department continue to champion non-toxic, ecologically responsible landscaping practices across the University of California system.

--- narrative ---
The Office of Sustainability at UC Berkeley led the data collection process for the STARS 3.0 report, utilizing various internal and external tools and platforms to gather and analyze data. This collaborative effort involved multiple campus departments, each providing essential input to ensure comprehensive reporting across all STARS categories.
1. Initial Planning and Stakeholder Engagement. The process began with strategic planning sessions to establish timelines, assign responsibilities, and clarify data requirements. Representatives from departments such as Facilities Services, Residential Life, Procurement, Energy and Utilities, and Academic Affairs joined the data collection team, working closely with the Office of Sustainability to set up a systematic approach for gathering information.
2. Data Collection and Verification. The Office of Sustainability distributed detailed data request templates, and each department identified primary contacts to gather specific data points. Data inputs from various departments were centralized in Shared Good Forms, organized by STARS sections to facilitate easy access and tracking. This centralized platform allowed departments to input data directly, improving efficiency and accuracy in data management.
3. Use of Analytical Tools and Platforms. In addition to the Shared Good Forms, a range of analytical tools and platforms were used to process and validate data. These tools supported efficient data handling, quality checks, and trend analysis, helping to ensure data accuracy before it was compiled into the report.
4. Consolidation, Cross-Referencing, and Supporting Evidence. The Office of Sustainability consolidated the submitted data into a central database, cross-referencing information across departments to ensure consistency. Each response was further supported with documentation, such as policies, procedural documents, and reports, to provide evidence for specific claims and metrics required by STARS.
5. Final Review and Submission. Once data consolidation and verification were complete, the Office of Sustainability conducted a final review with department leads and senior administration. Following this review, the completed report was submitted to AASHE. The final data will inform future improvements and sustainability planning at UC Berkeley.
Marina Zdobnova, Sustainability Data and Program Manager, Office of Sustainability
Ashley Smiley, Postodoctoral Fellow, Office of Sustainabiltiy
Several students were actively involved in the data collection process for the STARS 3.0 report. Their contributions included work on multiple sections including Climate Resiliency, Biodiversity studies, Student Engagement, and assisting with data validation.
The preparation of UC Berkeley’s STARS 3.0 report involved multiple layers of internal data quality control to ensure accuracy and consistency. Key controls included:
Departmental Review: Each department reviewed and verified its data submissions before sending them to the Office of Sustainability. This initial review helped to catch discrepancies and ensure data was accurate at the source.
Cross-Referencing: The Office of Sustainability cross-checked data across departments for consistency, particularly for complex metrics such as greenhouse gas emissions and energy usage, which may overlap across categories.
Documentation Requirements: Departments were asked to provide supporting documentation (e.g., policies, reports, procedural documents) for data points, which allowed for further verification and validation of reported data.
Centralized Data Collection Platforms: Data was gathered and managed using centralized platforms like Shared Good Forms, organized by section. This ensured that data entry was consistent and reduced the risk of errors in transferring information.
Final Review by Senior Staff: The compiled data underwent a final review by senior staff and administrators in the Office of Sustainability, who conducted a last check for accuracy and completeness before submission.

The American Cultures requirement is a Berkeley campus requirement, one that all undergraduate students at Berkeley need to pass in order to graduate. The requirement was instituted in 1991 to introduce students to the diverse cultures of the United States through a comparative framework. Courses are offered in more than fifty departments in many different disciplines at both the lower and upper division level.The American Cultures requirement and courses constitute a new approach that responds directly to the problem encountered in numerous disciplines of how better to present the diversity of American experience to the diversity of American students whom we now educate.Faculty members from many departments teach American cultures courses, but all courses have a common framework. The courses focus on themes or issues in United States history, society, or culture; address theoretical or analytical issues relevant to understanding race, culture, and ethnicity in American society; take substantial account of groups drawn from at least three of the following: African Americans, Indigenous peoples of the United States, Asian Americans, Chicano/Latino Americans, and European Americans; and are integrative and comparative in that students study each group in the larger context of American society, history, or culture.These courses focus upon how the diversity of America's constituent cultural traditions have shaped and continue to shape American identity and experience. This is not an ethnic studies requirement, nor a Third World cultures requirement, nor an adjusted Western civilization requirement, nor a course on racism.The American Cultures requirement meets the STARS definition of sustainability-supportive in the following ways:- Students will be able to demonstrate an understanding of the nature of systems.- Students will have an understanding of their social responsibility as future professionals and citizens.- Students will be able to accommodate individual differences in their decisions and actions and be able to negotiate across these differences.- Students will be able to analyze power, structures of inequality, and social systems that govern individual and communal life.- Students will be able to recognize the global implications of their actions.

The University of California further recognizes that by such policies, authors of scholarly articles can more easily and collectively reserve rights that might otherwise be signed away, often unnecessarily, in agreements with publishers. To accomplish this, authors take advantage of US copyright law to grant to the University a non-exclusive license (limited to the purpose of making the work openly available) for each scholarly article authored while employed by UC. Chancellor's Community Partnerships fund:
The UC Berkeley Chancellor’s Community Partnership Fund (the Fund) invests in projects and programs that establish new or existing partnerships between UC Berkeley and Berkeley community entities that improve the quality of life for people who live or work in Berkeley. The Fund seeks partnerships and collaborations that are designed to match the university’s expertise and service with community wisdom and leadership. Eligibility is limited to projects and programs that have at least one community partner and one UC Berkeley partner. An ideal campus/community partnership gives students, faculty and staff an opportunity to bring the university’s public service mission to life in community projects that further social justice and racial equity. Projects that benefit specific neighborhoods or groups of Berkeleyans are preferred.

Collaborative Structure:All projects must involve significant collaboration between UC Berkeley and at least one eligible community partner. Assessments emphasize the depth, sustainability, and potential for long-term impact of these partnerships.
Focus on Equity-Driven Policies:The assessment process prioritizes projects that align with equity-driven initiatives developed through extensive community engagement, such as the Berkeley Youth Equity Partnership. This ensures that projects directly address the needs of underserved populations.
Use of Assessment Results for Improvement:

UC approaches sustainability, at every stage of the investment process, both from a risk management and investment opportunity perspective. UC believes that a focus on sustainability, including in particular climate change, food and water security and the circular economy, can help create better risk-adjusted returns over the long term.
At the governance level, the UC Board of Regents’ Investment Policy Statement for the endowment makes clear that material ESG risks shall be considered with the same weight as any other material investment risk. The Board of Regents’ policy regarding sustainability in endowment investments states in relevant part:
“The Office of the Chief Investment Officer shall incorporate environmental sustainability, social responsibility, and governance (ESG) into the investment evaluation process as part of its overall risk assessment in its investments decision making. ESG factors are considered with the same weight as other material risk factors influencing investment decision making.
The Office of the Chief Investment Officer uses a proprietary sustainability framework to provide core universal principles that inform the decisions and assist in the process of investment evaluation. The Office of the Chief
Investment Officer manages the [endowment] consistent with these sustainability principles. The Framework can be found on the Office of the Chief Investment Officer website in the sustainability section.”
See: https://regents.universityofcalifornia.edu/policies/6102.pdf
At the Chief Investment Officer and staff level, all investment staff consider ESG factors in the investment process when such factors are material. Our Framework for Sustainable Investing describes our views on material ESG factors in more detail. See: https://www.ucop.edu/investment-office/sustainable-investment/sustainability-framework/index.html
To implement our approach, UC Investments has developed a range of practices. We manage our ~$23 billion endowment through external managers. The Director of ESG Integration conducts an ESG due diligence review of all potential new managers and investments, including in funds and co-investments. The ESG review one of the considerations in whether to proceed with a new investment.
UC Investments oversees and monitors the firms that manage our endowment in order to ensure that material ESG issues, if any, are being adequately addressed. Most of our external investment managers have best in class ESG staff and practices and are at the forefront of the investment industry’s efforts to integrate ESG considerations, including climate risk.

UC Investments uses negative screening to manage material risk to the endowment. We screen out the following from the endowment, across all asset classes (public equity, private equity, private credit, fixed income, real assets, real estate): fossil fuel, tobacco, private prisons, Sudan and firearms.
To accomplish this negative screening, we require our external public equity investment managers to track the MSCI ACWI IMI ex Tobacco ex Fossil Fuel Index, which screens out roughly 300 publicly traded companies that own “proved and probable” reserves of thermal coal, oil and gas and tobacco-related companies. We apply the same screening definition (i.e., any amount of fossil fuel reserves) to our other investments in public equities, fixed income and the private asset classes (private equity, absolute returns, real assets and real estate), thus comprehensively covering the entire endowment. To the extent that fossil fuel reserve owning companies are held in commingled accounts, UC Investments continues to reduce exposure by, for example, converting such accounts into separately managed accounts that exclude fossil fuel reserve owning companies.

The $39 billion UC Retirement Savings Plan (UCRSP) is the second largest in the USA, behind that of the federal government. When investing their defined contribution funds, employees may choose from an investment fund line up of 26 fund options, all of which negatively screen fossil fuel, tobacco, private prisons and firearms companies. The only way for participants to buy securities from these sectors is to opt out of the default options.
One of the 26 investment options offered to employees in the UCRSP provides a specific focus on sustainability - the UC Social Equity Fund, which seeks to track the performance of a benchmark index that measures the investment return of socially screened large- and mid-capitalization stocks. Like the other 26 investment options, the UC Social Equity Fund also negatively screens certain sectors, as described above.
For information on the UC Social Equity Fund visit: https://fwc.widen.net/s/rzbsk5bgkz/uc-social-equity

The following Statement on Sustainability is included in all 26 fund options:
“As reflected in the UCRSP Investment Policy Statement (IPS) of the UC Board of Regents, UC Investments incorporates “environmental sustainability, social responsibility, and governance (ESG) into the investment evaluation process as part of its overall risk assessment in its investments decision making. ESG factors are considered with the same weight as other material risk factors influencing investment decision making.” In cases in which the Chief Investment Officer finds that investment risks negatively impact investment returns, he may screen out certain companies or sectors. Such companies and sectors, include, for example, companies that manufacture civil firearms; operate private prisons; or own fossil fuel reserves. The UC Chief Investment Officer, in his fiduciary duty, has determined that companies that own fossil fuel reserves face, in the long run, strong secular headwinds for demand, continued price volatility and declining profitability. Removing fossil fuel reserve owning companies from the fund will reduce material risks and therefore have a positive long-term effect. This decision for the fund is consistent with the “de-risking” strategy already undertaken in the UC Pension and Endowment. In selecting and monitoring the type of companies included in the funds, the fund managers are responsible for complying with UC Investments’ screens. Mutual funds and collective investment trusts that include assets from other organizations are not subject to UC Investments' screens.”

The Higher Education Opportunity Act (HEOA) has provisions to reduce costs to students while supporting instructors' ability to select high-quality course materials. HEOA requires that course book information be made available to students before they enroll in a course. UC Berkeley complies with HEOA by asking faculty to submit book orders to the Cal Student Store even in cases where faculty place orders with independent bookstores, so they can be listed in the course catalogue. Providing book lists to the Cal Student Store, regardless of where books are ordered, also makes it possible for the store to buy back books at the end of the semester and offer them at a discount in the future.Link to rent textbooks from the Cal Student Store: https://calstudentstore.berkeley.edu/textbooksLibrary Pilot ProgramsIn cooperation with the Center for Teaching & Learning, Associated Students of the University of California, and Educational Technology Services, the Library launched a pilot for 2017-2018 to explore how we might reduce student costs for assigned course materials. The pilot tested, at limited scale, three Library-led services intended to help reduce the costs of assigned course content for Berkeley students, while also allowing the Library to gauge feasibility and efficiency were those services expanded.- Course Packs: The Library enabled instructors to create free and electronic course readers in lieu of instructors requiring students to purchase print copies from third-party vendors.- eBook Swaps: The Library acquired unlimited user licenses for books that the instructors would otherwise have required students to purchase in print.- Open educational resources (OERs): The Library and Center for Teaching and Learning supported instructors in shifting from traditional textbooks to OERs (online and free to read and reuse).The Library received financial support for our participation in the pilot programs from The Arcadia Fund.Over the course of the three pilot semesters (Fall 2017, Spring 2018, and Fall 2018), the Library supported over 40 courses, representing approximately 2400 students. While many scholars in the humanities and social sciences publish in OA journals, they also publish scholarly books, termed “monographs”. These books become a critical component of professional credentialing, yet their readership is limited by the same kinds of access barriers endemic to subscription-based journals: The scholarly books are quite expensive, and increasingly fewer libraries can afford to purchase them.University presses’ funding models for financing OA books are innovative and evolving. UC Press’ Luminos program (https://www.luminosoa.org/), for instance, is formulated as a partnership in which costs and benefits are shared by member organizations. Many academic publishers also offer a print or print-on-demand version of the book for sale to readers who prefer hard copies, further enabling cost recovery through traditional print sales. At their core, most OA book funding models typically charge academic authors the equivalent of an APC. Given the greater investment needed to create and edit longer and more complex manuscripts, the book processing charges (BPCs) can range upwards of $7000. At UC Berkeley, we can help subsidize those fees through our BRII program so that authors' out-of-pocket is zero or substantially reduced--with the upshot being that the resulting literature is available to all. Forbes' America’s Best Employers: UC Berkeley has appeared in Forbes' annual list of America’s Best Employers. This recognition is based on employee feedback and workplace assessments, acknowledging organizations that excel in various areas such as diversity, leadership, work-life balance, and employee satisfaction. Diversity Awards: UC Berkeley has received multiple awards for its commitment to diversity and inclusion. For example, the university has been recognized by organizations such as INSIGHT Into Diversity for its leadership in promoting a diverse and inclusive workplace through programs and policies that support equity and access. Sustainability Leadership: UC Berkeley is recognized as a leader in sustainability, both in its academic programs and its operational practices. The university consistently earns Platinum or Gold ratings from the AASHE STARS (Sustainability Tracking, Assessment & Rating System), which evaluates sustainability performance across various sectors, including operations, planning, and employee engagement. Healthy Campus Network: UC Berkeley is part of the University of California Healthy Campus Network, which is recognized for its efforts in promoting health and wellness for students, faculty, and staff. This initiative has received praise for its leadership in fostering a healthy work environment.
